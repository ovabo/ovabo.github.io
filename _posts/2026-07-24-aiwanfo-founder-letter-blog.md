Today we are launching Aiwanfo. We go into small and mid-sized businesses, build the AI they need, and secure it. We work in healthcare, manufacturing and warehousing, in the United States and West Africa.

This is the long version of why.

I grew up inside my family's business. My parents sell heavy equipment and the spare parts for it, and they run a technician service that hires out operators and technicians to the companies that need them. My mother ran a wholesale shop selling packaged food and household goods. I spent my childhood around machines, mechanics, inventory, and customers, in the kind of industry most technology people never look at.

In 2019 I started eQuipfy, an online marketplace for construction and heavy equipment in West Africa. It began on WhatsApp, and in 2021 we moved it onto its own platform. We built it with the team at Ovabor Labs, the company we run all our projects through, and Mayowa and Toluwani were part of it from the start. eQuipfy is still running today.

In February 2023 we started building AfriComply, software that helped African companies handle data security and privacy compliance. Nigeria's data protection law passed that June, a few months after we began, and we thought the timing was perfect. We were wrong about how the market would behave. We signed customers, but we never grew the way we expected. Enforcement was weak, and most companies worked out that paying a small fine cost far less than paying for a subscription every year. The problem was real and the software worked. The market was not ready to pay for it.

In April 2025 I stopped selling AfriComply. That was a hard month. I had spent two years on it, and I had to accept that I had built something people needed but would not buy yet.

By then I had been doing something else for over a year, and it is the reason this company exists.

We were adding AI to eQuipfy and working out what AI software for construction should look like. Construction, warehousing and food distribution all run on the same kind of physical operations, and across Nigeria and the rest of Africa, distributors and warehouses are everywhere. If we could build something that worked for one of them, it could work for the others. I did not want to guess at that from a laptop.

So from March 2024 I started taking operations jobs at night and on weekends while I ran the businesses during the day. The first one came out of a small annoyance. I had received four wrong deliveries in a row from a meal kit company, and I wanted to understand how a business that depends on precise execution keeps getting it wrong. I took a third shift job at their fulfillment center. During orientation I learned they were losing close to a million dollars to packing errors.

After that I worked at a foodservice distributor, first learning the operation and then on the floor as an order selector, where I got certified on the pallet jack and picked cases through the night. I also worked in warehouse IT during peak season. I wrote up what I saw each week so I could think through the patterns properly.

What I found changed what I wanted to build. I went in thinking about automation, and I came out much less interested in it.

Almost none of the errors were the fault of the person making them. A stack got mislabeled on an earlier shift and everyone downstream trusted it. Two ingredients looked identical at five ounces and seven ounces. A shelf got rearranged and someone reached from memory. At the distributor, a pallet stacked badly at nine at night cost a driver an hour the next morning, and one missed case turned into a customer refund and a mark against the picker. The people were doing the work well. The systems around them were not built to help them, and the software they had was designed for somebody else's operation.

The man who taught me most at the meal kit plant was on my team on the shipping line. He had been there two years and had helped open the plant, so he knew it from production through to quality assurance, and I asked him questions constantly. One night I caught an error before it went out, and I told him that software could catch that kind of thing, and that I was trying to build it. He thought about it and asked me what would happen to his job. He had been given a second chance and that job carried a great deal for him. I told him I was not building anything to replace him, and I meant it. Before I build now, I ask first whether it will help the person doing the work and their family.

In the summer of 2025 my own life came apart, and what I could not explain afterwards was how calm I had been while it happened. That question sent me reading into psychology and psychiatry, where I found a field with more open questions than most people realise. Psychiatry still diagnoses mostly from symptoms and self-report, without the objective tests the rest of medicine takes for granted.

So I did what I had already been doing and went to work in it. I took a job as a mental health worker on the overnight shift at an inpatient psychiatric hospital, doing rounds and documenting behavior on paper, while I kept building during the day.

Ovabor Labs exists to launch projects, and with eQuipfy running smoothly, Psykick became the next one. I started it on that psychiatric floor. It drafts clinical notes, prepares insurance paperwork, and appeals denied claims. A clinician reads and signs everything, and the software never makes a clinical decision. Mayowa led the engineering, Toluwani designed the product, and I worked as CTO and product manager.

We could not sell it to large hospital systems, because they already had a queue of companies calling them every week. So we went to private providers and independent practices, and that is where we learned the thing that led to this company. No single product fits every practice. Each one has its own workflow, its own payer mix, and its own rules. They did not need another product to buy. They needed someone to sit with them and build the right thing.

I have been building with the same two people for six years. Mayowa is a software engineer and studied computer science. Toluwani is a software engineer and product designer and studied computer science and economics.

I studied cybersecurity and computer science. I have worked across healthcare, from a consulting firm to an academic medical hospital to an inpatient psychiatric hospital, and across warehousing and food distribution. Earlier on I worked in finance, higher education, venture capital and software. Most of the operations work happened at night and on weekends while I ran our businesses during the day.

Between the three of us we have built a marketplace, a compliance product we had to close, and clinical software written from inside a hospital. We know how to ship, and we know what it costs to get it wrong.

In March 2026 Toluwani told me he was about to start his own design and AI consulting business. The plan we made was that he would design the next version of Psykick through his new company, and we would build it at Ovabor Labs with Mayowa and the rest of the team. That conversation went somewhere else. We kept returning to what the practices had already told us, which was that each of them needed something built for their own operation, and by the end of it I was telling him not to start alone and pitching the three of us on building that instead. Then on July 2 the gap I had been circling for a year became obvious to me.

This year the largest AI companies each set up their own teams to go into other businesses and build AI inside them. They committed roughly nine billion dollars to it in about two months. That tells you the approach works, because those companies do not spend that kind of money on a hunch.

Their customers are large enterprises, private equity portfolios, and startups with big investors. They work mostly in North America, Europe, Asia and the Middle East, and mostly for software companies. Small and mid-sized businesses were left out of it, and so was almost all of Africa.

Most business owners can see what AI could do for them. Many even know roughly how it would work. The big AI companies are simply not coming to help them.

So today we are launching Aiwanfo. We come into your business and build the AI you need, instead of selling you software and leaving you to work it out on your own.

We are starting with the industries I know from the inside, which are healthcare, manufacturing and warehousing. We work in the United States and in West Africa.

The way we work is straightforward. We sit with you and find where AI can genuinely help, and then we build it. We use closed and open models, whichever one fits your problem, your budget and your rules. We act as your advisor and your engineering team at the same time.

We also do the part most firms skip. AI costs are rising fast and companies are worried about their data and their security, so we build those protections in from the beginning and we secure the systems we deliver. The same systems everyone is rushing to put in place can be attacked. We build them to be safe, and we help you own your AI rather than rent it from someone else forever.

I came to this from a warehouse floor, a hospital ward, and a company I had to shut down. What I want is for small businesses, and for the places these companies ignore, to get the same benefit from AI as everyone else.

If your business is one of the ones that got left out, this company is for you.

Here is how to start. Write to me and we will spend an hour on your operation, at no cost and with nothing to sign. I will tell you where AI would help you, where it would not, and what it would take. If the honest answer is that you do not need us yet, I will say so.

We are also hiring engineers who want to build this way.
