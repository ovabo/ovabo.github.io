This morning I watched a psychiatrist assess a new admission. He walked into the room, asked the patient their name, asked about their parents, asked if they had any allergies. Then he asked the date. The year. The month. The patient answered each one. The psychiatrist said to restart them on their previous medications, and he walked out. The entire encounter took less than a minute.

I do not say this to criticize the psychiatrist. He has a full census to get through every morning, and the system rewards speed. But the patient had just been admitted to a locked psychiatric unit, and their first conversation with the person who controls their treatment plan lasted seconds. If there was something the patient needed to say that did not fit into "name, parents, allergies, date," there was no space for it.

As a mental health worker, I spend more direct time with patients than almost any other role in this hospital. That is not an opinion. It is the structure of the job. As a mental health worker, I do the 15-minute observation rounds. I do the one-to-ones. I sit with patients during crises. I take their vitals, measure their blood pressure, weigh and measure them during intake. I perform skin checks and inventory their personal belongings while the nurse assesses their condition. I monitor their CPAP machines at night. I respond to code blues, code greys, code reds. I transport them in ambulances to the ER when their bodies fail. I co-lead group therapy. I am the last face many of them see before they fall asleep, and the first face they see when they wake up. My job description says I perform direct and indirect psychiatric care to promote the comfort, safety and well-being of assigned patients. Almost every line of that job description ends with the same phrase: per hospital policies, per doctors order, with the supervision of the Charge Nurse.

My job is to see the whole person. My scope of practice is to tell someone else what I saw.

I have over 1,800 hours on locked psychiatric units. In that time, I have been floated across every unit type in the building: adolescents, adults, geriatrics, dual diagnosis. I have participated in dozens of admissions. I have ridden in the back of ambulances. I have watched a patient die. And the thing I keep running into is not that the system fails to see the whole person. The whole person is visible. The whole person is visible to the person standing closest to them. The system fails because the person who sees the most has the least authority to act on what they see.

---

A patient was having an episode on my unit. Staff were preparing to call a code grey. I decided to try to de-escalate first. I asked the patient why they thought they were having an episode. They told me it always happens before their menstrual cycle.

I took this to the charge nurse. The nurse said that was not correct.

But I knew it was a real phenomenon. Not because of a textbook, but because I had been seeing it for years on Nigerian social media. It is one of those recurring conversations in the +234 space. Women attribute their mood swings and irritability to their cycle, and men dismiss it as an excuse for bad behavior. A Nigerian pastor went viral in 2025 for preaching that women use their periods to justify anger. The feminist response was immediate and sharp, pointing out that premenstrual mood changes are hormonal, not character flaws. This is a debate that cycles through Nigerian social media constantly. When I heard the patient describe what was happening to her, I recognized it immediately. I had been watching people argue about it online for years. I later confirmed it in the DSM-5, where Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder appears under Depressive Disorders.[^1] It is a recognized category in the ICD-11. There is an entire organization, the International Association for Premenstrual Disorders, dedicated to it. They have a free self-screening tool. The patient was telling us exactly what was wrong. I reported exactly what the patient said. And the system dismissed it because the person reporting it was me, and the person who heard it decided it did not fit what they already believed.

I could not push back. It was outside my scope of practice. But that moment crystallized something I had been feeling for weeks. The patient had given us the answer. We did not listen because the answer did not come through the right channel.

This was not the only time. Another patient showed me some drawings once. Hidden in the corner of one sketch was a note. It said, "They made me do this, I don't really want to. I just need help." I wanted to ask more questions. I wanted to sit with the patient and understand what they meant. When I showed the nurse, they said the patient was just "buffing," which is floor slang for faking it.

The system had a category for the behavior but no process for the message. I saw something. I reported it. It was discarded. That is the cycle. Observe, report, watch it disappear into someone else's judgment.

---

In January, a patient I knew died in their bed with a CPAP mask on their face.[^2]

I had met this patient three times across three different units over the course of about a month. The second time, I was assigned as their CPAP watcher for the night. They barely slept. We talked most of the shift. They told me they did not like using the machine. It was uncomfortable. Hard to move around with. I sat with this patient for an entire shift, responsible for monitoring a device I barely understood. What I did not know was that the patient had a prior code blue on record. Nobody told me. The information that could have changed how I watched them that night was in a system I did not have access to.

The patient died weeks later, on a different night, on a different unit. The staff members who signed the rounds from 11:30 PM onward were fired. After the death, the hospital changed almost everything about how CPAP patients are monitored. New identification colors, new documentation requirements, licensed staff for one-to-one observation, pulse oximeters and flashlights at every bedside. Every single one of these protocols could have existed before. They required a death.

I wrote about the details of what happened and what changed in an earlier essay. What I did not write about then is what stays with me now. I saw this patient. I talked to them. I sat with them for hours. But the training I needed to understand their machine did not exist in my role, and the medical history I needed to protect them had not been shared with the person assigned to protect them. I could see the whole person. I could not keep them alive.

---

After the CPAP death, I thought about resigning. My original plan had been to leave after six months to free up time for studying, but for that week, the reason shifted. It was not about the schedule anymore. It was about whether I could carry this. I talked to my therapist about it. I talked to my friend who works in social work. I stayed. But something changed in how I think about where I am going.

I had considered becoming a licensed psychiatric technician, which is why I enrolled in the psych tech program in the first place. The LPT license would have given me a slightly wider scope. But the more I worked the floor, the more I realized that the scope was still too narrow. I would still be reporting to someone else. I would still be watching things I could not change. I looked at nursing as an option, but the nurses I work with spend most of their shifts at the computer, managing compliance documentation, intake prep, discharge paperwork, insurance billing. They need a doctor's order for almost everything. They are brilliant and overworked, and they still cannot make independent clinical decisions about the patients they are responsible for.

The wall I kept hitting was not about knowledge. I read the DSM-5 and the ICD-11 cover to cover. I study for the MCAT. I take pre-med courses while working overnight shifts. I understand more about these patients' conditions than my job title suggests. But understanding is not authority. On this floor, authority flows from licensure, and licensure flows from training. The only role that lets you both see the whole person and act on what you see, across the full range of what matters, the body and the mind and the social context, is the physician.

And not just any physician. A few months into the job, I rode in the back of an ambulance for the first time.[^3] The internist had noticed a patient's leg was swollen during rounds. He ordered blood work, the results raised a flag, and the decision was made to transfer the patient to the ER. In the ER, I watched the room fill with sensors. EKG leads, chest X-ray, ultrasound, CT scan. The clot was not in the swollen calf where everyone had been looking. It was high in the thigh, invisible from the outside. The CT scan found a pulmonary embolism. No amount of 15-minute rounds would have caught it. We needed the sensors.

But the ER doctor, armed with all of that technology, spent about forty seconds with the patient at the door. The psych unit had the human contact but lacked the sensors. The ER had the sensors but lost the human. The patient existed at the intersection of both, and neither system saw the full picture.

That ambulance ride is why I started looking into Consultation-Liaison Psychiatry. C-L is the specialty that operates between the psychiatric floor and the medical floor. The C-L psychiatrist gets called when a medical patient's behavior changes or when a psychiatric patient's body fails. You are forced to ask the question I keep asking on every shift: is this agitation caused by the disease, or by something the system is doing to the patient? Is the problem psychiatric, or is the liver failing, or is the room too loud, or did nobody listen when the patient said it happens every month before their cycle? C-L psychiatry does not get to choose one partition. It has to hold all of them at once.

I found the Academy of Consultation-Liaison Psychiatry's publications and read their guide to doing a consult.[^4] It described exactly what I had been doing informally since my first week on the floor. Look at the whole patient. Consider whether the symptoms are psychiatric or medical. Check the labs. Check the medications. Check the environment. Ask the patient what they think is happening. The difference is that the C-L psychiatrist has the authority to act on what that assessment reveals. I was already doing the observation. I just did not have the license to call it an assessment.

---

There is one more dimension to this that I cannot leave out, because it is personal.

I have seen my late father several times in my dreams since he died. In one vivid instance, I saw him sitting with me in my living room, and we were eating yam together. When I told my sister, she said she sees him sometimes too.

In the DSM-5, you could pathologize that as a hallucination. But research has shown that these experiences, sometimes called after-death communications, are common across cultures and actually help people process grief.[^5] In my Nigerian heritage, these experiences are not symptoms. They are relationships. The dead do not vanish. They visit. The diagnostic framework has no field for a continuing bond with the dead that is not a pathology.

If I were a patient instead of a worker, that dream could have been charted as evidence of a disorder. If I had described it during an intake that lasted less than a minute, there would have been no time to explain the cultural context. If the person I told decided it did not fit what they already believed, it would have been dismissed the same way the PMDD patient was dismissed, the same way the drawings were dismissed. The whole person would have been visible, but the part of me that makes sense of my father's death through the framework I was raised in would have been overwritten by a label from a book that does not account for it.

Miranda Fricker calls this testimonial injustice, the systematic downgrading of someone's credibility because of who they are.[^6] On this floor, I see it happen to patients constantly. A patient tells us what is wrong and we chart something else. A patient communicates through art and we call it faking. A patient describes a spiritual experience and we code it as psychosis. The whole person is right there. They are telling us who they are. We are just not listening, because the information is coming from a source the system does not trust.

---

I have been writing essays from inside this hospital since my first weeks on the floor. Each one has been about a different failure I observed. The ambulance essay was about the gap between psychiatric care and medical care. The locked unit essay was about the building making patients sicker. The entropy trap was about the environment fueling the disease. The CPAP essay was about a death that did not have to happen.

This essay is about why those are not separate problems. They are the same problem. The system has partitioned the patient across roles, departments, and frameworks that do not talk to each other. The psychiatrist holds one piece. The nurse holds another. The social worker, the mental health worker, the internist, the facilities team that controls the temperature and the lighting and the room assignments. Each piece reflects something real about the patient. But nobody is standing back far enough to hold all the pieces at once.

The whole person is not invisible. The whole person is visible to the people closest to the patient. The problem is that seeing is not enough. In this system, what you see only counts if you have the authority to act on it. And the people with the most contact, the most hours, the most observations, are the ones with the least power to change what happens next.

That is the observation problem. It is why I am going to medical school. Not to stop observing. To finally be allowed to act on what I have seen.
