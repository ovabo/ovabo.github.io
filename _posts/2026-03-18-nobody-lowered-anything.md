title: "Nobody Lowered Anything"
date: 2026-03-18
summary: "Five witnesses testified before Congress about why healthcare costs are rising. Each one told the truth about their piece of the system. Each one left out the truth about their own role in breaking it."
---

Last week we ran a unit of 40 patients with three nurses and five mental health workers. Two of the mental health workers were assigned to one-to-one observations, which meant they could not leave their patients. That left three of us to cover rounds, respond to codes, handle admissions, and manage every other patient on the floor. The charge nurse was at the computer for most of the shift. The other two nurses were passing meds and prepping paperwork. Nobody was available to sit with the patient who had been crying quietly in the hallway for twenty minutes.

I have written about understaffing before. I used to think it was a management problem. Something the hospital could fix if it chose to. But I have been learning the policy architecture underneath the floor I work on, and what I have found is that the staffing levels I see every night are not a failure of planning. They are the predictable output of a behavioral health service line that operates at negative financial margins.[^1] The hospital cannot hire more staff for a unit that loses money on every patient it admits.

On March 18, 2026, the House Energy and Commerce Subcommittee on Health held a hearing titled "Lowering Health Care Costs for All Americans: An Examination of the U.S. Provider Landscape." Five witnesses testified. Rick Pollack, president and CEO of the American Hospital Association. David Aizuss, chair of the American Medical Association Board of Trustees. Shawn Martin, CEO of the American Academy of Family Physicians. Elizabeth Mitchell, president and CEO of the Purchaser Business Group on Health. And Anthony DiGiorgio, a neurosurgeon at UCSF and a health policy researcher who testified in his personal capacity.

I read all five testimonies. Each one was detailed, well-sourced, and persuasive. Each one described a system in crisis. Each one told the truth about their piece of it. And each one left out the truth about their own role in breaking it.

The AHA testimony opened with hospitals under financial pressure. Pollack described an industry where labor costs account for roughly 60 percent of total operating expenses, supply spending increased 9.9 percent in 2025, and drug expenses increased 13.6 percent.[^2] Medicare underpayments to hospitals totaled over $100 billion in 2024, and Medicare margins have dropped to negative 12 percent.[^3]

The sharpest section was about insurers. Pollack reported that hospitals spent over $43 billion in 2025 just trying to collect payment that insurers already owed them for care that had already been delivered.[^4] One mid-sized health system employs roughly 200 people to manage prior authorizations and denials for inpatient services alone. Of those 200, ten are physicians who do nothing but argue with insurers to secure authorization for patient care. Another mid-sized system spends more than $9 million annually obtaining prior authorizations and more than $13 million appealing denials, the vast majority of which are overturned.[^5]

I read that and thought about the patient on my floor who waited nearly a month for a transfer from the ICU to the DDMI unit. The unit that would help was in the same building. The paperwork was the barrier.

Pollack also noted that behavioral health, obstetrics, trauma care, and burn services all operate at low or negative margins. That single line, buried in the middle of a 13-page testimony, explains more about my floor than anything else in the document. It is the reason we are understaffed. It is the reason the hospital eliminated licensed psychiatric technician and licensed vocational nurse positions years ago and replaced them with unlicensed mental health workers. It is the reason they only brought those positions back after a patient died.

But the AHA testimony left things out. It did not mention that many nonprofit hospitals maintain multi-billion-dollar investment portfolios. It did not mention executive compensation packages that run into the millions. It did not mention the hospital industry's aggressive consolidation over the past two decades, which multiple studies have shown raises prices without improving quality. It did not mention the backlash that nonprofit hospitals have drawn for spending on advertising campaigns, including one that drew criticism for running a Super Bowl ad while patients struggle with bills.[^6] The AHA framed hospitals purely as victims of insurer practices and Medicare underpayment. That is true. But it is not the whole truth. Hospitals are also actors in this system, and some of the cost pressures they describe are the consequences of their own growth strategies.


The AMA testimony came from the physician side. Aizuss described a workforce being crushed by stagnant payment, rising costs, and regulatory burden.

The numbers are difficult to absorb. Medicare physician payment rates have increased approximately 10 percent over the past 25 years. During that same period, the Medicare Economic Index, which measures inflation in physician practice costs, increased approximately 63 percent.[^7] When you adjust for the actual cost of running a practice, Medicare physician payment has effectively declined by about 33 percent since 2001. In 2015, services paid under the Physician Fee Schedule represented about 38 percent of total Medicare Part B spending per enrollee. By 2025, that share had dropped to 28 percent.[^8] For every $100 Medicare spends on a fee-for-service enrollee, only $28 goes to physician services.

The administrative burden compounds the financial pressure. It costs an estimated $12,811 and more than 200 hours per physician per year to comply with MIPS, the quality reporting system that is supposed to tie payment to outcomes.[^9] Nearly 90 percent of physicians report that prior authorization contributes to burnout.[^10] Medicare Advantage plans alone made nearly 53 million prior authorization requests in the most recent reported year.[^11]

The AMA also described the workforce crisis. Over 75 million people live in Health Professional Shortage Areas. The projected shortage is up to 86,000 physicians by 2036.[^12] Part of the reason is the cap on Medicare-funded residency slots, which has been frozen at 1996 levels for three decades. Medical schools have expanded enrollment, but the residency pipeline has not kept pace. In the most recent match, over 9,500 applicants went unmatched.

The AMA testimony argued that raising the GME cap and supporting international medical graduates are essential to closing the gap. There is truth in this. Approximately 25 percent of licensed physicians in the United States are international medical graduates, and they disproportionately serve areas with high rates of poverty and chronic disease.[^13] But the position deserves more scrutiny than the AMA gave it.

The GME cap was introduced in the 1997 Balanced Budget Act. The AMA supported it at the time, warning of a "physician oversupply." That warning turned out to be wrong, and the cap has constrained the residency pipeline ever since. The AMA now advocates for raising it. But the organization asking Congress to fix the cap is the same organization that supported creating it.

The IMG question is also more complicated than the testimony suggested. IMGs fill critical gaps, particularly in inner-city safety-net hospitals and primary care specialties that US graduates often avoid. During COVID, nearly half of IMGs were supporting rural patients.[^14] But the system as it stands creates a real tension. The number of residency slots is fixed. Every slot an IMG fills is a slot a US-trained graduate does not get. US medical students carry an average of $200,000 to $300,000 in debt. Many IMGs trained in countries with heavily subsidized medical education and carry little or no educational debt, starting their careers on a fundamentally different financial footing. And the claim that IMGs serve underserved areas long-term is complicated by evidence that many do not remain in those areas once their visa obligations end.[^15]

I am an African immigrant. I came to this country over a decade ago. I have a personal stake in a system that allows international graduates to contribute to American healthcare. But I also study for the MCAT alongside US students who are accumulating enormous debt for the chance to match into a pipeline that has not expanded in 30 years. The problem is not IMGs. The problem is that the cap forces a zero-sum competition between qualified physicians who should all have a path to practice. The AMA is right that the cap needs to be raised. But the AMA helped create the cap. That history should be part of the testimony.


The AAFP testimony shifted the lens to primary care. Martin described a system that chronically undervalues the one part of healthcare that consistently lowers costs and improves outcomes.

Ninety percent of the nation's $4.5 trillion in annual healthcare spending is associated with people living with chronic and mental health conditions.[^16] Primary care is the most effective and least expensive way to manage these conditions. Patients with a regular primary care physician have 54 percent lower total healthcare expenditures for chronic disease and 20 percent lower odds of hospitalization.[^17] But the United States spends less than five cents of every healthcare dollar on primary care, and that share has been declining. In Medicare, it is 3.4 cents.[^18]

The consequence is that nearly a third of American adults report having no usual source of care, the highest number in a decade.[^19] One in three Americans is cutting back on daily spending to cover medical costs. People are skipping meals and putting off buying homes or having children to keep up with healthcare bills.[^20]

Martin described how financial pressure has transformed primary care from independent practices into an employed workforce. Twenty years ago, about a third of family physicians were employed. Today, nearly 75 percent work for hospitals, health systems, or corporate entities including private equity.[^21] Independent practices cannot survive on payment rates that have not kept pace with costs, cannot negotiate effectively with consolidated insurers, and cannot absorb the compliance burden of quality reporting programs.

Martin also described downcoding, a practice in which insurers retroactively reduce the billing code on a claim without consulting the physician who provided the care. The AAFP has asked insurers for transparency into the criteria they use. To date, no insurer has provided it.[^22]

The AAFP testimony was compelling. But it also presented primary care physicians purely as victims of a system that undervalues them. It did not address the fact that fee-for-service creates volume incentives for all providers, including independent ones. It did not address the variation in practice patterns among primary care physicians, some of which drives unnecessary spending. And it did not address why, if primary care produces such clearly superior outcomes, the profession has not been more effective at organizing politically to change its own payment structure over the past three decades. The underinvestment in primary care is real. But the profession's own fragmentation is part of why the underinvestment has persisted.



The PBGH testimony came from the employers who pay for healthcare on behalf of over 180 million Americans. Mitchell described a system that is not responsive to the people who fund it.

The annual cost to provide healthcare to an American family has risen an average of 6.1 percent per year for two decades, reaching over $35,000 last year.[^23] Many PBGH members are experiencing double-digit increases with no corresponding improvement in quality.

The most striking finding was about hospital pricing. Using the transparency data that Congress required hospitals to publish, the PBGH analyzed prices across multiple markets and found no correlation between hospital price and quality or safety.[^24] None. Hospital prices have risen 256 percent between 2000 and 2024, while overall inflation rose only 87.3 percent.[^25] The prices are not explained by outcomes. They are explained by market power. Eighty-two percent of metropolitan areas have one or two health systems controlling 75 percent of the market.[^26] One PBGH member removed a single overpriced hospital from their network and saved an estimated $120 million over four years.[^27]

Mitchell also presented data showing that advanced primary care models reduce total costs by 20 percent while improving outcomes. But most health plans have declined to adopt or scale these models, often citing the difficulty of reallocating funding away from hospitals.[^28]

The PBGH testimony was the most data-driven of the five. But employers are not purely innocent bystanders. Employers have spent decades outsourcing healthcare purchasing decisions to the same insurers and PBMs they now criticize for being unresponsive. Many have tolerated opaque contracting arrangements, accepted annual premium increases without demanding quality data, and resisted the administrative complexity of direct contracting even when the savings are demonstrated. The PBGH members who are innovating are the exception. Most large employers have not done this work. The system is unresponsive to employers in part because employers have not consistently demanded responsiveness.


The fifth witness was Anthony DiGiorgio, a neurosurgeon at UCSF's Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital and Trauma Center.[^29] He has a Master of Health Administration from LSU and completed a health policy fellowship in the U.S. Senate for Senator Bill Cassidy. He is affiliated with the Philip R. Lee Institute for Health Policy Studies and the Mercatus Center at George Mason University. He testified in his personal capacity. His testimony was the most structurally critical of the five because he did something the others did not. He named consolidation as the root cause, not just a contributing factor.

DiGiorgio presented the concentration data. Of 389 metropolitan areas, only five are not highly concentrated for hospital care by FTC standards.[^30] Multi-hospital systems owned 81 percent of hospital beds in 2020, up from 58 percent in 2000. Physician practice ownership has dropped from 76 percent in the 1980s to 35 percent in 2024.[^31] In five major subspecialties, only 12 percent of physicians are not affiliated with a hospital, corporate entity, or private equity.

The price effects are documented. Monopoly hospitals charge 12 percent more than hospitals in competitive markets. Hospital mergers in concentrated markets have raised prices by as much as 65 percent, including at neighboring hospitals.[^32] Hospital acquisition of physician practices is associated with a 14.1 percent increase in prices for those physicians' services.[^33] And the quality justification for consolidation does not hold up. One study found that hospital mergers were associated with higher inpatient mortality among patients with heart disease. Another found no significant improvement in readmissions or mortality after hospital acquisitions.[^34]

DiGiorgio also named a consequence that none of the other witnesses mentioned. Hospital mergers suppress wages. Skilled healthcare workers see a 4 percent drop in wages after a concentration-increasing merger. Nurses see a nearly 7 percent drop.[^35] And rising hospital prices function like a tax on the broader economy. A one percent increase in healthcare prices lowers payroll and employment at non-healthcare employers by 0.4 percent.[^36] Americans whose raises never arrive are the hidden victims of hospital pricing power. Their compensation is being diverted into health benefits they did not ask for and cannot negotiate.

DiGiorgio's proposed solution was to restore physician-led entry into the market by repealing the ACA's Section 6001, which froze physician-owned hospital expansion in 2010. He cited a colleague's independent spine hospital that charges 50 to 80 percent of what neighboring hospitals charge, operates with about a third of the total staff, and has a patient satisfaction score of 93 out of 100 while comparable hospitals hover in the mid-teens.[^37] Federal law prevents this hospital from expanding to meet demand from Medicare patients.

DiGiorgio's testimony was the most direct about root causes. But physician-owned hospitals are not automatically the answer to consolidation. The Stark Law and anti-kickback statutes exist because physician self-referral was a real problem. Physicians who own the facilities they refer patients to have a financial incentive to refer more, not less. DiGiorgio acknowledged this and suggested that an exception to Stark could be structured within managed care settings where utilization review provides a check. That is a reasonable position. But the broader argument that physician ownership is inherently more efficient and patient-centered requires the same scrutiny that every other witness's claims require. Ownership structure does not guarantee alignment with patient interests. Incentives do. And fee-for-service creates misaligned incentives regardless of who owns the facility.



I read all five testimonies in one sitting. The pattern was immediate.

The hospitals say they are drowning because insurers deny claims and Medicare underpays. They do not mention their own consolidation, their executive compensation, or their investment portfolios. The physicians say they are drowning because Medicare payment has declined 33 percent and the regulatory burden is crushing independent practice. They do not mention that the AMA supported the GME cap that created the residency bottleneck. The family physicians say primary care is drowning because it gets less than five cents of every dollar. They do not address the profession's own fragmentation or the volume incentives that fee-for-service creates for all providers. The employers say they are paying double-digit increases with no quality improvement. They do not mention that most of them have spent decades accepting opaque contracting terms without demanding accountability. The neurosurgeon says consolidation is the root cause and physician-led entry is the answer. He does not fully address the self-referral incentives that led to the regulations he wants repealed.

Every one of them told the truth about their partition. Every one of them left out the truth about their own role in the architecture.

This is the same pattern I see on my floor. The psychiatrist writes orders based on a chart reviewed for seconds. The nurse documents compliance because the billing codes demand it. The social worker arranges discharge during business hours. The mental health worker does the overnight rounds but cannot act on what they observe. Each one operates from their own partition and believes they have the situation covered. The system fails because the partitions do not communicate and because none of them examines their own contribution to the failure.

What none of the five witnesses mentioned is the floor. Not one described what happens inside a locked psychiatric unit at 3 AM when the census is 40 and there are three mental health workers covering rounds. Not one described the patient who waited a month for a transfer while paperwork cycled between the hospital and the regional center. Not one described the mental health worker monitoring a CPAP machine they were never trained on, attached to a patient whose cardiac history they were never given. Not one described the nurse who spends the shift at the computer because documentation requirements demand it, while a patient cries in the hallway.

The testimony described the architecture. I live inside it. The costs they are trying to lower are the reason my unit is understaffed. The prior authorizations they all agree are broken are the ones that delay my patients' care. The physician workforce shortage they are trying to solve is the pipeline I am trying to enter. The administrative burden they want to reduce is the reason the nurse is at the computer instead of at the bedside.

I learned most of this in my introductory healthcare sector class before I read the testimonies. But seeing it presented to Congress made it concrete in a different way. In class, you learn the theory. On the floor, you see the output. In the hearing room, you watch the people who built the system explain why it is failing, and you realize that each of them is holding one piece of a problem that none of them can solve alone. They are not wrong about their piece. They are just unwilling to examine their own.

The hearing was titled "Lowering Health Care Costs for All Americans." Nobody lowered anything. Each witness explained why their partition is underfunded, overburdened, or exploited by someone else. The architecture stayed exactly the same. And tonight, the floor will look the same as it did last week. Forty patients. Three nurses. Five mental health workers. A patient crying in the hallway. And nobody available to sit with them, because the service line that funds their care operates at a loss.
